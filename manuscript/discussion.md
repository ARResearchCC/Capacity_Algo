## 4. Discussion

The three sizing methods differ far less in what they cost than in what a planner can trust them to deliver. On annualized total system cost they are effectively tied — the between-method spread is smaller than the fold-to-fold weather noise in every climate, valuation, and architecture — so the practically meaningful distinctions lie in reliability, and specifically in whether a design's out-of-sample performance matches the performance it was planned for.

### 4.1 Why the common heuristics mis-generalize, and what a calibrated design gives a planner

The average-year design is optimistic. Because it averages the capacities that are individually optimal for each training year, it settles near the central tendency of those designs; and since the capacity a year demands rises disproportionately in adverse years, that central design systematically under-provisions for the tail, promising a reliability it does not deliver: delivered loss of load exceeds its planning estimate in every cell (median test-to-plan unmet ratio 1.11). The common thread is that reliability is governed by a heavy adverse tail, so any design pinned to a single summary year — central (LP-Avg) or extreme (LP-Worst) — is miscalibrated, whereas a design that integrates the tail (CVaR) matches the distribution's shape. A planner who trusts its planning number will systematically under-provision and be surprised by adverse years. The worst-year design fails in the opposite direction. Anchored to the single most expensive training year, it over-provisions (median ratio 0.51; delivered unmet roughly half of planned) and buys reliability it will rarely need with capital it always pays for. Neither heuristic is wrong about the years it saw; both are miscalibrated about the years it has not. The risk-aware CVaR design is the one whose promise holds — its delivered reliability tracks its planning estimate closely (median ratio 1.02; best-calibrated in 26 of 30 cells, the exceptions confined to the tropical climate; pooled train-to-test unmet degradation +7.6%, versus +17% for the average-year design). That CVaR is built to weight the adverse tail makes this partly true by construction, but the substantive finding is that the design intent survives honest out-of-sample cross-validation while the deterministic heuristics do not. For a planner, calibration is the property that actually matters: not the lowest number on a design slide, but a reliability figure that survives contact with unseen weather.

### 4.2 What risk aversion buys, and at what cost

Beyond calibration, risk aversion narrows the two distributions a resilience planner cares about most. Relative to the average-year design, the CVaR design cuts the worst-fold loss of load by about 21% and the year-to-year cost variability by about 31%; the tail reduction grows with the value placed on lost load, from roughly 12% at the low valuation to nearly 48% at the high. This advantage is not simply the dividend of using stochastic optimization: compared directly against the risk-neutral stochastic program (λ = 0) within the same cross-validation, the risk term alone cut out-of-sample unmet load by a median 40% and its tail by a median 19% in every one of the fifteen PCM cells tested, for a median 1.2% cost premium, so within this architecture the benefit is attributable to risk aversion rather than to the switch to stochastic optimization (this λ=0 comparison covers the PCM architecture only; the PV–battery case is left to future work). What it does not buy is a lower expected cost. On the risk-neutral total-cost metric the average-year design is marginally cheapest, and CVaR sits a small, bounded premium above it — under about 1.5%, with a median below half a percent — while remaining below the worst-year design in every cell. This ordering is exactly what a risk measure should produce: a risk-averse objective trades a little expected cost for a smaller tail, and it would be cause for suspicion, not celebration, if the same design also minimized the mean. Part of the tail reduction is itself paid for with a modestly larger system (about 1% more total spend, roughly 5% more capital, offset by lower expected penalty), so the *less confounded* benefit is the reduction in year-to-year cost variance — a more predictable annual outlay. Because part of this reduction reflects shifting spend from the weather-variable penalty into deterministic capital, an equal-capital control (matching the average-year design's capital to the CVaR design's) would be needed to fully isolate the risk-allocation component (see Limitations). The honest characterization is therefore not that risk-aware planning is cheaper, but that it is comparable in cost, more trustworthy in its reliability, and more stable in its year-to-year economics.

### 4.3 PCM as a battery substitute, and storage and planning as coupled decisions

The second design lever, PCM thermal storage, acts principally as a substitute for the battery rather than for PV. Adding matched hot and cold PCM leaves PV capacity within a few percent but displaces 38–86% of battery energy — the largest displacement in the continental climate — and because latent storage is cheaper per kilowatt-hour than electrochemical storage for the thermal loads it serves, total system cost falls by 4–14%. This coupling matters for planning because thermal flexibility and the sizing method are not independent choices: the CVaR/PCM design lies on the cost–reliability efficient frontier in all five climates, whereas its PV–battery counterpart does not. We are careful not to overstate this. Frontier membership is largely an architecture-level effect — PCM shifts the whole frontier outward, so all PCM designs tend to dominate all PV–battery designs — rather than evidence that risk-aware sizing is uniquely enabled by PCM, and the reliability margin of CVaR over the average-year design widens with PCM in only three of five climates. The defensible claim is narrower and, we think, more useful: treating storage technology and planning method as coupled decisions, rather than optimizing one and then bolting on the other, yields designs that are simultaneously efficient and calibrated.

### 4.4 Deployment implications

For deployment, the economic case against diesel does not rest on the contested in-theatre fuel prices that motivate expeditionary energy programs. The renewable microgrid breaks even against an all-diesel genset at a delivered fuel price of only $0.78–8.90/gal across climates and architectures — at or below ordinary commercial diesel in four of the five climates, and even in the subarctic worst case below routine forward-delivered fuel and an order of magnitude below protected-convoy cost. The renewable system is therefore economically preferable wherever fuel must be delivered at any meaningful premium, and the risk-aware design secures that economic case while also bounding the reliability tail. That combination is what a mission-critical or remote site requires: an adverse year is precisely when service must not fail and when resupply is least assured. Because the fully renewable model carries no dispatchable backup, the site is never left unpowered, and the break-even needs no assumed fuel price; the value placed on lost load enters the comparison only through the renewable design's small residual thermal-unmet penalty (negligible in the warm climates, larger in the penalty-heavy subarctic case), so the diesel comparison is essentially decoupled from the demand-side reliability valuation.

### 4.5 Limitations

Several limitations bound these conclusions. First, the between-method differences, though strikingly consistent in direction — CVaR improves on the average-year design's out-of-sample unmet in every cell tested — are in most single cells smaller than the fold-to-fold weather noise; we therefore report the consistent direction across cells rather than claiming per-cell significance, and a formal paired test that respects the dependence among cells (they reuse the same 25 weather years across valuations and architectures) has not yet been applied. Second, loss of load is almost entirely thermal: critical and electrical demand is near-must-served under the high critical valuation, so the entire cost–reliability tradeoff lives in HVAC comfort load, and in the subarctic climate the heating load is so large relative to achievable renewable supply that it is structurally unservable by any method — there, method choice is second-order. Third, the risk-parameter robustness check covers only the PCM architecture (five climates by three valuations), and the risk preference (λ = 0.9, α = 0.9) is fixed a priori as a stated posture for a mission-critical base, not tuned to the data; at N ≈ 20 training years, α = 0.9 places only two scenarios in the CVaR tail, a small-sample estimate. Fourth, the reliability tail is measured through a coarse worst-of-five-folds proxy — the maximum of five multi-year means — which understates the noise inherent in a true tail statistic; a per-year tail metric over the 25 realizations would be sharper. Fifth, the tail-reliability and cost-variance gains are partly confounded with the CVaR design's larger capital stock (about 5% more capital than the average-year design); an equal-capital control has not yet been run, so the pure risk-allocation effect is not fully separated from the capacity effect, and the λ=0 head-to-head that isolates the risk term covers only the PCM architecture. Sixth, both the contiguous-block cross-validation and the equiprobable-scenario CVaR treat the 25 weather years (1998–2022) as exchangeable draws from a stationary climate; a monotone interannual trend would bias both, and the series has not yet been tested for one (e.g. a Mann–Kendall/Sen-slope check on PV yield and heating/cooling load). Finally, the study rests on a single small deployable-installation load archetype evaluated at five sites, and both the value of lost load and the delivered diesel price are assumptions rather than measurements. These last two are addressed by the reliability-valuation sensitivity and the price-independent break-even, respectively, but a materially different load profile or cost environment could shift the quantitative balance — while, we expect, leaving the qualitative ordering intact: calibrated, trustworthy reliability at comparable cost.
