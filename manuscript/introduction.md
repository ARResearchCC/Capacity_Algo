1. Introduction
Energy security increasingly depends on the ability of power systems to maintain critical services during extreme weather, infrastructure failures, and other disruptions. These risks create a need for resilient, localized energy systems that can support facilities during grid outages or operate in locations without reliable grid access. Microgrids strengthen critical-infrastructure resilience by coordinating local generation, energy storage, and demand and maintaining service when the surrounding power system is disrupted [1]. The United States Department of Energy defines a microgrid as a group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity and can operate either with or independently from the main grid [2]. These capabilities are particularly important for remote communities and mission-critical facilities, where prolonged power interruptions can have severe consequences and access to external energy resources may be limited.
Diesel-powered microgrids remain the incumbent solution for many remote communities and expeditionary military facilities. Diesel generators are dispatchable, technologically mature, and deployable with relatively limited supporting infrastructure [3,4]. Their apparent reliability, however, depends on continuous fuel availability. Transportation and protection requirements increase the delivered cost and environmental burden of diesel in isolated locations, while disruptions to fuel supply chains can directly threaten energy availability [3,5]. At expeditionary bases, fuel-resupply activities can also expose personnel and logistical assets to risk and divert resources from mission activities [3]. Diesel systems additionally require recurring maintenance and careful generator dispatch because inefficient loading and cycling increase fuel consumption and operational burden [4]. Diesel generation therefore does not eliminate energy-security risk; it transfers a substantial portion of that risk to fuel logistics, equipment availability, and sustained maintenance.
The primary problem addressed in this study is whether a fully renewable islanded microgrid can provide reliable electrical and thermal service under uncertain future conditions while remaining economically competitive with diesel-based power. Renewable generation and energy storage can reduce dependence on transported fuel, lower life-cycle emissions, and improve local energy self-sufficiency [5,6]. Recent studies further indicate that renewable microgrids can be economically favorable relative to diesel in some remote applications [6,7]. Eliminating diesel backup, however, transfers the reliability challenge from fuel availability to the variability of renewable generation and energy demand. Solar availability, electrical demand, and heating and cooling loads vary across hours, seasons, and years, while generation and storage technologies have finite power and energy capacities [7,8]. A system designed using favorable or limited historical conditions may therefore experience loss of load during adverse weather years, whereas a system designed around the most extreme observed conditions may require excessive investment. The practical challenge is thus to design a renewable microgrid that is dependable enough to serve critical loads and affordable enough to build.
This study addresses the reliability–cost challenge through two complementary approaches. The first is a planning approach: using CVaR-based stochastic optimization to account explicitly for interannual uncertainty and severe unmet-load outcomes. The second is a technology approach: integrating hot and cold phase-change-material thermal energy storage to increase the flexibility with which heating and cooling demands are served. Improved planning may prevent capacities from being determined by an unrepresentative historical year, while PCM storage may reduce the amount of electrical generation and battery capacity required to maintain thermal service during constrained periods. The central premise examined in this study is that risk-aware planning, PCM integration, or their combination may improve out-of-sample reliability without making the renewable system prohibitively expensive.
The first gap concerns how the capacity planning of renewable islanded systems balances computational tractability against reliability and cost under interannual uncertainty. Optimal sizing of standalone and hybrid renewable systems is a mature field, and reviews of the area show that most studies minimize levelized or life-cycle cost subject to a reliability index such as loss-of-power-supply probability, using heuristic, metaheuristic, or software-based tools that are typically exercised on a single representative year of weather and load data [28]. Such formulations remain tractable partly because they compress interannual variability into a single historical year, a typical meteorological year, hourly averages, or a small set of representative days [28, 31]. However, temporal reduction can materially change generation and storage decisions by smoothing demand peaks, masking prolonged renewable shortages and extreme conditions, and distorting chronological storage behavior [9–12], and single-year formulations have been shown to misestimate both the sizing and the reliability of off-grid systems exposed to multi-year variability [31]. A design optimized on a narrow set of conditions may therefore fail under unseen weather years, while sizing around a single extreme year may lead to unnecessary overinvestment [7]. These effects are especially consequential for a permanently islanded system, which has neither a grid connection nor, in the fully renewable case, a dispatchable fuel-based generator to absorb an adverse year.
Explicitly representing this uncertainty in the sizing problem is therefore essential, and several families of methods do so. Robust and distributionally robust optimization protect capacity decisions against unfavorable realizations or against ambiguity in the underlying probability distributions [13, 14], and have been applied to stand-alone microgrid sizing by minimizing load-shedding or shortfall risk [24]; depending on how the uncertainty or ambiguity set is constructed, however, they can become computationally demanding or produce conservative designs. Conventional stochastic optimization is less conservative and instead optimizes expected performance across scenarios. Macmillan et al., for example, recast a single-year deterministic microgrid design model as a two-stage stochastic program over multi-year climate, load-growth, and renewable scenarios to minimize expected long-term cost [8]. An expected-value objective, however, can assign insufficient weight to the infrequent but consequential loss-of-load events that dominate the risk facing a fully renewable islanded system, and such formulations commonly retain a dispatchable backup generator to absorb the residual tail.
Conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) offers a tunable middle ground by letting the planner define the adverse tail of the outcome distribution and control how strongly those outcomes influence capacity decisions. CVaR has been applied to integrated energy-system and energy-hub planning with renewables and storage [15], to risk-averse capacity planning with peer-to-peer trading in multi-energy microgrids [25], and to integrated charging-microgrid planning under seasonal uncertainty [17], and it is widely used in microgrid operation and scheduling, including energy-storage control and islanded dispatch under renewable uncertainty [26, 27]. The applications closest to the present work size islanded systems directly: one optimizes an islanded multi-energy microgrid for a cold climate while reporting CVaR across scenarios [32], and another embeds CVaR in a two-stage stochastic program for resilient microgrid storage placement [33]. Collectively, these studies establish CVaR as an effective means of incorporating adverse outcomes into energy-system decisions. Yet the capacity-planning studies are predominantly grid-connected, market-participating, or interconnected multi-energy systems, in which CVaR chiefly hedges cost or profit; where islanded systems are sized, CVaR tends to enter as a post-hoc evaluation metric [32] or to hedge operational-cost tails within a siting and reconfiguration problem [33], rather than as the objective that drives greenfield capacity under interannual weather uncertainty. The operation and scheduling studies, in turn, do not determine long-term technology capacities. Limited evidence therefore exists on whether CVaR-based capacity planning can improve the out-of-sample reliability–cost balance of a fully renewable, permanently islanded microgrid whose principal risk is unmet critical electrical and thermal load under unseen weather years, and in which flexibility is supplied by battery and phase-change thermal storage rather than by fuel backup or grid exchange.
The second gap concerns the contribution of PCM thermal storage to the out-of-sample reliability and cost of an optimized renewable microgrid. Phase-change materials store and release large quantities of latent heat over a narrow temperature range and offer roughly two to three times the volumetric energy density of sensible storage, and reviews of heat-pump-integrated PCM systems show that this enables thermal load shifting, peak-demand reduction, and greater operating flexibility across air-, ground-, dual-source, and solar-assisted configurations [29, 30]. At the component and demand-flexibility level, PCM has been used to shift heat-pump electricity consumption and enhance building demand flexibility [18], to improve peak-shifting performance in latent-heat and cascade heat-pump systems [19, 20], and to shift air-source heat-pump loads through integrated storage [22], with recent work also demonstrating dedicated cold-side PCM storage for cooling applications [21]. These studies consistently show that realized performance depends on the PCM transition temperature relative to the heat-pump operating range, on charging and discharging rates, thermal conductivity, and heat losses, and on how the storage is integrated with the heat pump [30, 19–22]. PCM therefore represents a technically mature means of adding both hot- and cold-side thermal flexibility to an electrified heating and cooling system [29, 21].
Applications of PCM within complete microgrids, as opposed to single buildings or components, are more limited but are beginning to emerge. Verdugo et al. modeled the detailed operation of a thermoelectric microgrid with a PCM thermal system, establishing its value at the dispatch level [23], while Mühlbauer et al. incorporated PCM into the multi-decadal cost minimization of islanded renewable-electricity microgrids across several climate zones [7]; islanded multi-energy sizing studies have likewise begun to include thermal storage alongside electrical storage [32]. The present study is most closely related to Mühlbauer et al. [7] but differs in both objective and evaluation: that work minimizes multi-decadal system cost, whereas this study applies risk-aware planning that targets the adverse tail of unmet-load outcomes and tests every resulting design on unseen weather years. That combination is what allows the reliability contribution of PCM to be isolated — whether adding matched hot and cold PCM storage lets a design developed from historical data maintain lower electrical and thermal loss of load under unseen conditions than an otherwise identical system without PCM, rather than only reducing cost. It remains unclear whether PCM chiefly lowers cost, lowers loss of load, or shifts the cost–reliability frontier, and in particular whether the additional thermal flexibility it provides enables risk-aware planning to outperform simpler sizing methods by a wider margin than in a system without PCM.
To address these gaps, this study sizes both architectures — the PV–battery–heat-pump system and its counterpart with hot and cold PCM storage — using three planning methods: an average-capacity benchmark (the mean of the capacities optimized independently for each historical year), a conservative highest-cost-year benchmark, and CVaR-based stochastic optimization. The risk-neutral limit of the same stochastic program (λ=0) is also carried through as a scientific baseline, so the contribution of the risk term can be isolated from that of stochastic optimization per se. Every resulting design is evaluated out of sample by five-fold cross-validation over 25 years of weather and load data across five climate zones, with a diesel microgrid as the economic reference. Three questions guide the analysis:
Does CVaR-based planning yield designs whose out-of-sample reliability is better calibrated to the planning estimate — delivering the loss-of-load performance they were sized for, rather than systematically under- or over-provisioning — and whose tail loss of load and year-to-year cost variability are lower than the average-capacity and highest-cost-year benchmarks, at comparable annualized total system cost (capacity and operating cost plus value-of-lost-load penalties for unmet electrical and thermal demand)?
Does adding hot and cold PCM storage improve out-of-sample cost–reliability performance, and does coupling it with risk-aware sizing yield a design that lies on the cost–reliability efficient frontier across climates, where the corresponding PV–battery design does not?
Under which climate, reliability, and delivered-diesel-price conditions does a carefully designed fully renewable microgrid provide a reliable and economically competitive alternative to diesel?
The study's central contribution is a calibration finding. Evaluated honestly on unseen weather years, the sizing heuristics commonly used for islanded systems are miscalibrated — the average-year design systematically under-provisions and the worst-year design over-provisions — whereas a risk-aware CVaR design delivers close to the loss-of-load performance it was sized for, at comparable annualized cost and with a lower reliability tail and year-to-year cost variability. Comparing CVaR directly against the risk-neutral limit of the same stochastic program (λ=0) shows this advantage is attributable to risk aversion, not to stochastic optimization alone. As a secondary, architecture-level contribution, matched hot and cold PCM storage displaces battery capacity and shifts the cost–reliability frontier outward across climates, and embedding it within the risk-aware problem yields designs that are simultaneously efficient and calibrated. More broadly, the work strengthens the technical and economic basis for fully renewable islanded microgrids as practical replacements for diesel in remote and mission-critical applications.
