## 3. Results

Across every climate, architecture, and reliability valuation, the three sizing methods separated less on *how much* they cost than on *whether the reliability they promised survived contact with unseen weather*. Loss of load was almost entirely thermal: critical electrical unmet load was negligible in every cell (worst case under 1 kWh yr⁻¹, a single California–PCM realization), because the high critical value of lost load acted as a near-hard service constraint. The cost–reliability trade-off therefore lived entirely in the flexible HVAC demand, and all reliability figures below should be read as thermal loss of load. Method-to-method differences were, in most cells, smaller than the year-to-year (fold-to-fold) weather noise; we accordingly emphasize the *direction* of each effect and its consistency across cells rather than any single-cell magnitude, and note where a gap sat within ±1 fold standard deviation.

### 3.1 Sized capacities

The three methods populated a clear risk spectrum in system size: the worst-year benchmark (LP-Worst) built the largest systems, the average-year benchmark (LP-Avg) the smallest, and the risk-aware stochastic program (SO-CVaR) an intermediate design (SI capacities figure). In the cost-dominant cold climates SO-CVaR's storage sizing tracked cheap LP-Avg rather than LP-Worst's over-build; in Alaska, for instance, battery capacity rose only marginally from LP-Avg to SO-CVaR before jumping by roughly a third at LP-Worst. LP-Worst was also the least fold-stable of the three, carrying the largest sizing variance across folds on nearly every component. Adding PCM displaced battery capacity, not PV: PV capacity moved by at most a few percent when PCM was introduced, whereas battery capacity collapsed by 38–86% across climates (largest in Minnesota, roughly −86%). The optimizer also recovered the physically correct thermal regime endogenously — hot PCM in the heating-dominated climates, cold PCM in the tropics, both in the arid climate. Full per-component capacities are reported in the SI.

### 3.2 Out-of-sample cost: parity, not saving

On mean out-of-sample total cost the three methods were effectively tied (cost figure). SO-CVaR carried a small premium over LP-Avg in every one of the ten climate–architecture cells at medium VoLL, ranging from +0.21% (Minnesota–PVB) to +1.49% (Florida–PVB), with a median of roughly +0.45%; LP-Avg was the marginal cost minimizer everywhere. Against LP-Worst, SO-CVaR was cheaper in all ten cells, by up to −3.3% (Alaska–PVB). Crucially, these method gaps were smaller than the across-fold cost variability in every cell — in Alaska the fold standard deviation reached roughly 13% of mean cost against a three-method spread of only a few percent — so on total cost alone the methods were not separable given fold-to-fold weather variability. The mechanism was that LP-Worst bought robustness with capital rather than with lower total cost, while SO-CVaR selected an intermediate capital point. The result establishes the premise for everything that follows: SO-CVaR's reliability advantages came at *comparable* cost, and the method was in no sense cheaper than LP-Avg.

### 3.3 Out-of-sample reliability calibration — the lead result

The decisive difference among the methods was calibration: whether the reliability a design delivered on unseen years matched the reliability it was sized for (calibration figure). We measured this with the ratio of test-year to planning-year unmet load, for which a well-calibrated method sits at 1.0. SO-CVaR was the only method that kept its planning promise, with a median test-to-plan unmet ratio of 1.02 and a mean absolute deviation from unity of 0.14. LP-Avg systematically *under-provisioned* — its designs delivered more loss of load out of sample than planned (median ratio 1.11, mean absolute deviation 0.27), and it did so in all 30 cells. LP-Worst systematically *over-provisioned*, delivering roughly half the loss of load it planned for (median ratio 0.51, mean absolute deviation 0.45), wasting capacity in all 30 cells. On the per-cell deviation |ratio − 1|, SO-CVaR was the best-calibrated of the three methods in 26 of 30 cells; the four exceptions were all in the tropical climate, where absolute unmet load is driven close to zero and interannual percentage variability is largest, so a deterministic heuristic happened to sit closer to unity (SO-CVaR's ratio there rose to ≈2.1 and LP-Avg's to ≈2.6). SO-CVaR's median ratio nonetheless remained 1.02, so those cells inflate its mean absolute deviation without changing the median ranking. This speaks directly to whether the designs merely overfit 25 weather years: among the three deployed methods, the common heuristics did — the average-year design promised a reliability it did not deliver, and the worst-year design paid for reliability it did not need — whereas the stochastic risk-aware design was calibrated. Whether this calibration is specific to the risk term or a property of stochastic optimization in general is taken up in §3.4, where SO-CVaR is compared directly against the risk-neutral (λ=0) program. Because reliability here was thermal, this calibration statement is a statement about delivered HVAC service; cost calibration, by contrast, reversed (LP-Avg was best), which is why the trustworthy-reliability claim is confined to the reliability axis.

### 3.4 Risk reduction: tail loss of load and cost variance

Beyond matching its planned mean, SO-CVaR compressed the adverse tail of both reliability and cost relative to the risk-neutral LP-Avg (risk figure). Worst-fold unmet load fell by a median of 21% against LP-Avg, in all 30 cells, and the reduction scaled with the reliability valuation — roughly 12% at low VoLL, 20% at medium, and 48% at high — consistent with a method that weights adverse years more heavily as those years are priced more dearly. Year-to-year (across-fold) cost variability fell by a median of 31%, again in all 30 cells. An honest caveat applies to the tail-reliability result: SO-CVaR's larger intermediate design carried about 1% more total mean spend than LP-Avg (roughly 5% more capital, largely offset by lower expected penalty), so part of the tail reduction was bought rather than purely a risk-allocation effect. The cleaner, unconfounded win was the cost-variance reduction, which is not a mechanical consequence of higher mean spend and which held uniformly across cells. The worst-of-five-folds statistic is itself a coarse tail proxy, so these magnitudes are best read as consistent directional evidence rather than precise tail quantiles.

A natural objection is that this risk reduction merely reflects the use of *stochastic optimization*, not the *risk term* itself. To isolate the risk term we compared SO-CVaR directly against the risk-neutral two-stage stochastic program (λ = 0) — the natural scientific baseline — within a common cross-validation over the weather years (risk-term figure; PCM architecture, 15 climate × VoLL cells). Relative to the risk-neutral design, SO-CVaR lowered mean out-of-sample unmet load in all 15 cells (median −40%) and worst-fold unmet in all 15 (median −19%), the reduction again scaling with the reliability valuation, for a median out-of-sample total-cost premium of only 1.2%. The risk term therefore improves out-of-sample reliability over a proper risk-neutral stochastic baseline — not only over the deterministic heuristics — at a bounded cost, which locates the benefit in risk aversion rather than in stochastic optimization alone.

### 3.5 Diesel break-even

Placed on a common economic footing, the renewable microgrid undercut an all-diesel generator at a delivered fuel price of only $0.78–8.90 per gallon across all climates and architectures (diesel figure), a price-independent break-even that does not require an assumed fuel price and that carries no diesel dispatch decision on the renewable side. Four of the five climates broke even at or below ordinary commercial diesel (roughly $3–5 per gallon): Florida at $1.07 per gallon (PCM $0.78), Arizona $1.40, California $1.69, and Minnesota $3.78; only Alaska, at $8.90 (worst fold ≈ $9.9), approached the range of a real pump price, and even there the break-even remained well below routine forward-delivered fuel (≈ $13/gal) and an order of magnitude below contested-theatre cost. The order-of-magnitude climate gradient was driven by renewable capital under weak polar insolation rather than by diesel economics. PCM lowered the break-even in every climate, amplified relative to its 4–14% total-cost effect because the saving acts on the fuel-only numerator. No contested-theatre fuel premium was needed to justify the renewable design.
