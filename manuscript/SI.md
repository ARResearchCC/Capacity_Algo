S1. Climate sites and weather data
Five sites are selected to span distinct Köppen–Geiger climate zones: California (temperate/marine, Csb), Alaska (subarctic, Dfc), Arizona (dry/hot desert, BWh), Minnesota (humid continental, Dfa/Dfb), and Florida (tropical, Am/Aw) [34]. For each site, hourly weather data — global horizontal, direct normal, and diffuse horizontal irradiance; ambient temperature; wind speed; and relative humidity — are obtained from the U.S. National Solar Radiation Database for the 25 years 1998–2022 [35]. Each of the 5 sites × 25 years = 125 location-years is processed independently, preserving both seasonal structure and interannual variability.

S3. Passive building thermal model
Net building thermal load at each timestep is computed by a reduced-order passive heat-transfer model calibrated for the FOB structure [38], assuming a constant indoor setpoint. Net heat transfer is the sum of conductive, convective, radiative-cooling, and (daytime) solar-heat-gain terms:

QcondQ_{\text{cond}} Qcond​ and QconvQ_{\text{conv}} Qconv​ are driven by the indoor–ambient temperature difference and wind speed; QsolarQ_{\text{solar}} Qsolar​ is the incident solar gain modulated by a solar-heat-gain coefficient (SHGC); and QradcoolQ_{\text{radcool}} Qradcool​ is the radiative-cooling loss modulated by a radiative-cooling coefficient (RCC). The SHGC and RCC values are not fixed but are selected at each timestep from calibrated day/night parameter sets by matching current conditions to the nearest cluster centroid (the SHGC clustering uses wind, humidity, temperature, cloud cover, irradiance, and clearness index; the RCC clustering uses wind, humidity, temperature, and cloud cover). A positive QnetQ_{\text{net}} Qnet​ is served as cooling and a negative QnetQ_{\text{net}} Qnet​ as heating. Full calibration and clustering details are in [38].
S4. Stochastic electrical load model
Critical electrical load for the FOB is generated by an occupancy-driven Monte Carlo model. An occupancy schedule is first drawn for each of up to four occupants; lighting, computer-charging, and equipment (e.g., kettle) loads are then sampled at each timestep from occupancy-conditional probability distributions (e.g., computer-charging load ∈ {0, 0.5, 1.0} kW, kettle load ∈ {0, 0.133, 0.266} kW, with occupancy-dependent weights). Peak lighting load is 0.1 kW, applied when occupied; the FOB critical electrical load comprises lighting, computer-charging, and kettle loads (the 0.1 kW plug load is not part of the FOB scenario). A distinct random seed is assigned to each of the 25 years, so load realizations differ year to year while remaining reproducible.
S5. System architectures and techno-economic parameters
Both architectures use a heat pump with heating and cooling coefficient of performance COP = 3.5 and a fixed electrical rating of 10 kW. The battery has an inverter efficiency of 0.98, a maximum depth of discharge of 0.8, a self-discharge of 0.01/24 per hour, and charge/discharge power limited to 0.25 C. The hot and cold PCM stores (second architecture only) are modeled as energy reservoirs charged and discharged by the heat-pump and cooling loops.

S8. Capacity-planning formulations
All models share the same hourly dispatch physics — energy balances for PV, battery, heat pump, PCM, and load; storage state dynamics; and power/energy capacity limits — and differ only in how interannual uncertainty is handled.
LP-Avg. A deterministic linear program minimizing annualized capacity cost plus VoLL penalties is solved independently for each training year; the reported design is the arithmetic mean of the per-year optimal capacities.
LP-Worst. The same per-year LPs are solved, but the design is taken from the single training year with the highest total cost (a conservative, worst-observed-year design).
SO-CVaR. A two-stage stochastic program treats each of the NN N training years as a scenario s with shared first-stage capacities and scenario-specific dispatch. Let ZsZ_s Zs​ be the annual VoLL-weighted unmet-load cost in scenario ss s. The objective is

where the bracketed term is the CVaR of the outage cost at confidence α\alpha α, linearized following Rockafellar and Uryasev [44] (η\eta η is the value-at-risk auxiliary variable and ξs\xi_s ξs​ the tail excess). S9. Cross-validation procedure
The 25 weather years are partitioned into five contiguous five-year blocks. In each of the five folds, one block (5 years) is held out for testing and the remaining 20 years are used for training. Capacities are determined on the training years by each planning method, then held fixed and re-simulated on the held-out test years. Reported metrics are averaged over test years within a fold and over the five folds. For each design and test year, we record total system cost and its components, and loss-of-load metrics for both electrical and thermal service: total loss-of-load hours, number of distinct loss-of-load events, and the duration of the worst single event.
S10. Diesel baseline
Configuration. A diesel generator supplies all electricity, including the electrical demand of the same 10 kW heat pump (COP 3.5); there is no PV, battery, or PCM. Because diesel dispatch has no storage and hence no intertemporal coupling, sizing and dispatch are computed directly without an optimizer.

Delivered fuel price. The delivered ("fully burdened") fuel price is varied over three levels representing the range of in-theater resupply conditions: $13/gal (peacetime forward ground delivery), $45/gal (protected convoy), and $400/gal (contested / remote resupply). The upper tier lies within the JASON in-theater FBCF estimate of $100–$600/gal [45], the Deloitte energy-security analysis [46], and the AEPI convoy-casualty study underlying the force-protection component of FBCF [47].
