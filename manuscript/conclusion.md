## 5. Conclusion

This study asked whether the sizing methods used for fully renewable, permanently islanded microgrids actually deliver, on unseen weather years, the reliability they were planned for — and whether a risk-aware objective yields trustworthy, calibrated reliability at comparable cost. Every design was judged out of sample for a forward operating base across five climate zones and 25 weather years (1998–2022), for a PV–battery–heat-pump system and an otherwise identical system with hot and cold phase-change-material (PCM) thermal storage. Two deterministic benchmarks — the average-capacity and the highest-cost-year heuristic — and the risk-neutral limit (λ=0) of the stochastic program were compared against the conditional-value-at-risk (CVaR) formulation. The study's central contribution is thus a calibration finding — that common islanded-sizing heuristics are miscalibrated on unseen years while a risk-aware design keeps its promise — with PCM/planning coupling a secondary, largely architecture-level contribution.

The three research questions are answered as follows. **RQ1:** CVaR-based planning does *not* yield a lower out-of-sample total cost than the average-year benchmark — it carries a small mean-cost premium (+0.2 to +1.5%, median +0.45%), though it remains below the worst-year heuristic in every climate. What it buys at that comparable cost is reliability that can be trusted: the loss of load a CVaR design delivers on unseen years matches what it was sized for (median test-to-plan unmet ratio ≈ 1.02), whereas the average-year design systematically under-provisions (1.11) and the worst-year design over-provisions (0.51). CVaR is also substantially less risky than the average-year design, cutting the worst-fold unmet by about 21% and year-to-year cost variability by about 31% in all cells tested; and, tested against the risk-neutral stochastic program itself (λ = 0) in the PCM architecture, the risk term lowered out-of-sample unmet by a median 40% (and its tail by 19%, 15/15 cells) for a ~1.2% cost premium — so the benefit is attributable to risk aversion, not merely to using stochastic optimization. Loss of load is almost entirely thermal; critical electrical demand is near-must-served throughout. **RQ2:** adding PCM lowers total system cost by 4–14% by displacing battery capacity (38–86%) rather than PV, and coupling PCM with risk-aware sizing places the design on the cost–reliability efficient frontier in every climate, where the corresponding PV–battery design is not — although this frontier membership is partly an architecture effect and should be read as such. **RQ3:** the renewable microgrid undercuts a diesel genset at a break-even delivered fuel price of roughly $0.78–8.90/gal in every climate — far below in-theatre resupply costs and, in four of five climates, at or below ordinary commercial diesel.

Several directions follow. The risk-neutral (λ = 0) comparison shown here for the PCM architecture on reliability level and tail should be extended to the PV–battery architecture and to the train-vs-test calibration axis, which would complete the isolation of the risk term from stochastic optimization (a one-line re-run of the sizing model). A per-year tail-risk metric (CVaR of realized cost) would sharpen the tail characterization beyond the coarse worst-fold proxy used here; a paired significance test respecting the dependence among cells would formalize the direction-of-effect claims; and hourly dispatch traces would expose the operational mechanisms behind the sizing outcomes. Broader load archetypes and additional sites would test generality, and treating the value of lost load as endogenous under deep uncertainty would let the reliability–cost balance itself respond to mission conditions.
