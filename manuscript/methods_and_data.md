2. Methods and data
This study evaluates fully renewable islanded microgrids for a forward operating base (FOB) and benchmarks them against a diesel-generation baseline. Two matched renewable architectures — a solar photovoltaic (PV)–battery–heat-pump system, and an otherwise identical system that adds hot and cold phase-change-material (PCM) thermal storage — are each sized with three capacity-planning approaches and stress-tested against a quarter-century of interannual weather and load variability across five climate zones. The main text presents the modeling logic and the elements specific to the study's contribution; the full data-generation pipeline, all equations, and all numerical parameters are given in the Supplementary Information (SI).

2.1 System architectures and costs. 
Both architectures serve the FOB's critical electrical load together with its heating and cooling demand. The baseline pairs PV and battery storage with a single electric heat pump serving both heating and cooling; the second architecture adds hot and cold PCM stores that charge and discharge through the heat-pump and cooling loops, decoupling heat-pump operation from instantaneous thermal demand (Figure 1). In both, the PV, battery, and — where present — hot and cold PCM capacities are the decision variables, while the heat pump is fixed at a nominal 10 kW electrical rating (SI Section S5); in the coldest climate, peak heating can exceed this, contributing to the structural thermal shortfall discussed in Section 4. Capital costs are annualized with a capital-recovery factor and combined with fixed operating costs; unmet load enters the same cost basis through its value of lost load (Section 2.3). Component ratings, techno-economic parameters, and the annualization model are given in SI Sections S5–S6.
2.2 Weather, resource, and load data. 
Five representative U.S. sites spanning distinct Köppen–Geiger climate zones — temperate/marine, polar, dry, continental, and tropical — capture the range of solar and thermal conditions a deployable system may face [34]. For each site, hourly meteorological data for 1998–2022 are drawn from the U.S. National Solar Radiation Database [35], giving 5 sites × 25 years = 125 location-years that preserve both seasonal structure and interannual variability. A physics-based pipeline converts each location-year into three time-synchronized inputs: a PV profile computed with pvlib using the Fuentes cell-temperature model [36,37]; heating and cooling demand from an experimentally validated reduced-order passive heat-transfer model [38]; and a critical electrical load profile from an occupancy-driven probabilistic model, seeded distinctly per year for reproducibility (SI Sections S1–S4).
2.3 Capacity-planning approaches. 
Reliability is valued economically rather than imposed as a hard constraint: unmet electrical and thermal load are penalized at their respective value of lost load (VoLL), creating a direct trade-off between capital investment and the cost of service shortfalls [39]. The electrical VoLL is held well above the thermal VoLL to reflect the primacy of critical electrical service over flexible HVAC demand; both are guided by the LBNL Interruption Cost Estimate Calculator and the microgrid VoLL literature [40–43] and varied across low, medium, and high levels as a sensitivity (SI Section S7). Three approaches then size each architecture, sharing identical hourly dispatch physics — energy balances for PV, battery, heat pump, PCM, and load; storage state dynamics; and power and energy limits — and differing only in how interannual uncertainty enters the sizing decision. The average-capacity linear program (LP-Avg) solves a deterministic cost-minimizing LP independently for each training year and adopts the mean of the per-year optimal capacities; the conservative benchmark (LP-Worst) adopts the capacities from the single most costly training year; and the risk-aware stochastic program (SO-CVaR) treats each training year as a scenario s with shared first-stage capacities and scenario-specific dispatch, minimizing annualized capacity cost plus a convex combination of expected and tail outage cost:

2.4 Out-of-sample evaluation and diesel benchmark. 
Out-of-sample performance is assessed by five-fold cross-validation over the 25 weather years, partitioned into five contiguous five-year blocks. In each fold, capacities are fixed on the 20 training years and re-simulated on the five held-out years, isolating behavior under unseen conditions (SI Section S9). The primary metric is annual total system cost — annualized capacity cost plus VoLL penalties for unmet load (the fuel-free renewable systems carry only fixed O&M, folded into annualized capacity, and no variable dispatch cost) — reported with the duration and severity of electrical and thermal loss of load (total hours, number of distinct events, and worst single-event duration), averaged over test years and folds. We additionally quantify each design's reliability calibration by the ratio of delivered (test-year) to planned (training-year) unmet load, which equals one when out-of-sample reliability matches the planning estimate: for each design, method, and fold the chosen capacities are re-simulated on the 20 training years (planned unmet) and on the five held-out years (delivered unmet), and we report the median ratio and the mean absolute deviation |ratio−1| across cells (SI Section S9). Two risk statistics are also reported per cell: the worst-fold unmet load (a coarse tail proxy) and the across-fold standard deviation of total cost (year-to-year cost stability). A diesel-only system serving the identical load over the same sites and years provides the economic reference: a generator supplies all electricity, including the same heat pump, with no PV, battery, or PCM. Because diesel dispatch has no storage and hence no intertemporal coupling, its sizing and dispatch are computed directly rather than by optimization, with capacity set to peak demand plus a reserve margin and fuel use following a standard load-dependent generator model (SI Section S10). Since delivered fuel dominates remote diesel costs, the delivered price is varied across the fully burdened cost of fuel (FBCF) for in-theater resupply, from secure rear-area to contested front-line conditions [45–47]. To place the two system types on a common footing, we also report the breakeven delivered diesel price at which diesel total cost equals that of the best renewable design; a breakeven below plausible delivered fuel costs indicates the renewable design is already the cheaper option.
